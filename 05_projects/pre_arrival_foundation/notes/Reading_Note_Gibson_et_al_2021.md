# Reading Note: Gibson et al. (2021)

## Header

- **Citation key:** GibsonEtAl2021
- **Full reference:** Gibson, C.B., Gibson, S.C. and Webster, Q. (2021) 'Expanding our resources: Including community in the resource-based view of the firm', *Journal of Management*, 47(7), pp. 1878-1898. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206320987289
- **DOI / stable URL:** https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206320987289
- **Source type and weight:** Peer-reviewed journal article; high-weight theoretical source.
- **Pages read:** pp. 1878-1898 (PDF pp. 1-21).
- **Project relevance:** A strong starting point for Resource-Based View (RBV), corporate-community engagement, CSR, sustainability, stakeholder relations, and international-business contexts.

## Facts

- **Purpose:** The authors argue that a firm's community can be treated as a potentially valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable strategic resource, and ask how that expansion of RBV contributes to theory, research, and practice (pp. 1878-1880).
- **Theory and concepts:** RBV/VRIN characteristics; stakeholder perspective; corporate community investment; community engagement; social impact; interest alignment; cultural competencies.
- **Method and evidence base:** This is primarily a conceptual/theory-building article. It reviews relevant literature, uses paired company illustrations, and reports a structured search of 85 highly rated business and management journals. The search returned 905 articles, was narrowed to 111, and two raters retained 25 articles directly relevant to RBV and community as a sociogeographical resource (p. 1894).
- **Main argument:** The article maps community relationships onto RBV's valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable characteristics, proposing that durable, context-specific relationships and community capabilities may benefit firms and communities simultaneously (pp. 1884-1893).
- **Research agenda:** The authors state that theory specifying the mechanisms of corporate-community engagement needs further empirical substantiation and recommend richer designs, including longitudinal studies, stakeholder feedback forums, qualitative interviews, case studies, and randomised controlled trials where appropriate (pp. 1891-1892).

## Analysis

### Contribution

The article broadens a firm-centred RBV by placing community relationships inside the strategic-resource discussion. Its most useful contribution is not proof that community engagement causes performance, but a mechanism-oriented proposition: direct, durable engagement may produce relational pathways, trust, interest alignment, and social capital that create both firm-level and community-level value.

For Management and International Business, the international relevance is credible but conditional. The article repeatedly notes that multinational firms operate across distinctive local communities and that standardised programmes may fail when they ignore local demographic and cultural contexts (p. 1888). This offers a useful bridge between RBV and context-sensitive international management.

### Limitations and cautions

1. The article does **not** test a causal effect of community engagement on sustained competitive advantage. Its corporate comparisons are illustrative examples, not a controlled empirical design.
2. 'Community' is broad and potentially difficult to operationalise consistently across local, online, indigenous, employee, customer, and host-country settings.
3. There is a normative tension: treating a community as a strategic resource may conflict with recognising it as a stakeholder with independent claims. The authors themselves argue that communities may be both (p. 1890), which needs careful treatment rather than a purely instrumental interpretation.
4. The article acknowledges that common corporate-community indicators are coarse and that evidence linking engagement to impact is often limited (pp. 1891-1892).
5. Case claims should be traced to the underlying cited primary or empirical sources before being relied upon in assessed writing.

### Critical use

Use this source to frame and critique an argument, not as the sole evidence base. Pair it with: (a) foundational RBV work, (b) empirical studies of stakeholder/community engagement or performance, (c) institutional theory for host-country and legitimacy pressures, and (d) research that questions the business-case assumptions of CSR.
